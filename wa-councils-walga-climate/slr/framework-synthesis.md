# Framework synthesis

Objective 1, Step 3. What the eight included studies (`included-studies.csv`)
assume about the strength of the instrument they study, and what has to
change before any of it can be applied to WALGA's Western Australian Local
Government Declaration on Climate Change.

## The gap, now search-supported rather than assumed

`EXECUTION_PROMPT.md` stated, from a handful of scoping searches, that no
existing study addresses WALGA's Declaration specifically. The documented
search behind this synthesis (`search-protocol.md`, `prisma-log.csv`) found
37 records across six identification searches, including a search run
specifically for "WALGA declaration on climate change" together with
academic, study or thesis. None returned a study of WALGA's Declaration.
That confirms the gap for this pass. It remains provisional until the
Scopus and Web of Science re-run (see `search-protocol.md`) is done, because
a general web search can miss records that sit in an indexed database
without a strong public web presence, for example a thesis or a conference
paper.

## What the six declared-instrument studies assume

Six of the eight included studies (S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6) study a formal
**climate emergency declaration**: a council resolution that names an
emergency, typically alongside language about disrupting normal operations
or fast-tracking action. Every one of their frameworks carries that
assumption somewhere in its design, and none of the six frameworks was
built with a weaker, non-emergency instrument in mind.

- **S2 (Bush and Doyon 2025)**, the transformative-action framework, scores
  a council's follow-up plan against five elements: mitigation and
  adaptation together, cross-council collaboration, links to a biodiversity
  emergency, First Peoples' knowledges, and justice and equity. Nothing in
  the five elements requires an emergency framing by name, so the elements
  themselves could transfer. What does not transfer is the framework's
  starting premise, that there is a declaration event serious enough to
  expect a dedicated follow-up plan at all. WALGA's Declaration carries no
  such expectation; a signatory council may fold its commitments into an
  existing strategic community plan rather than issue a standalone plan.
  **Adaptation needed:** treat "is there a dedicated follow-up plan" as one
  scored item among several, not a precondition for using the rest of the
  framework.

- **S5 (Greenfield, Moloney and Granberg 2022)**, the symbolic-act-versus-
  disruption framing and its 10-attribute checklist, explicitly asks
  whether a council's response reaches "genuine emergency-mode governance,"
  defined in part by whether climate is prioritised over other council
  functions. That bar assumes the instrument itself asserts an emergency in
  the first place. WALGA's Declaration does not assert an emergency (WALGA,
  n.d.), so grading a signatory against an emergency-mode bar would
  penalise it for not doing something it never committed to. **Adaptation
  needed:** keep the 10 attributes as a checklist of what a mature climate
  response contains, but drop "reaches emergency-mode governance" as a
  pass/fail criterion; report attribute coverage on its own terms instead.

- **S4 (Hicks, Davidson, Lau et al. 2025)**, the PALLM "climate emergency
  mode" detector, is the closest methodological precedent to this
  project's own design: it compares declared and non-declared councils'
  policy text over time using an automated pipeline. But the construct it
  detects, "climate emergency mode," is defined by the presence of
  emergency-specific language and framing. Applying that detector directly
  to WALGA-signatory councils would most likely return near-zero scores
  for everyone, signatory and non-signatory alike, because none of them use
  emergency language. That would look like "no effect" when the real
  finding might be "the tool is measuring the wrong thing." **Adaptation
  needed:** keep the before/after, declared-versus-comparison design, but
  replace the "emergency mode" construct with content items that a
  Declaration signatory could plausibly commit to: adaptation and
  mitigation targets, named responsibility, resourcing, and reporting
  cadence, drawn from the coding items below.

- **S3 (Gudde et al. 2021)**, the delivery-plan completeness check, is the
  most directly transferable of the six, because "does a plan with targets,
  funding and a timeline exist" does not depend on the instrument naming an
  emergency. **Adaptation needed:** minimal; this check becomes one coding
  item in the adapted framework rather than the framework's central
  question.

- **S6 (Howarth, Lane and Fankhauser 2021)**, the pathway-and-purpose
  framework, explains why a council signs rather than what changes
  afterwards, so it answers a different question from this project's RQ2
  and RQ3. It is still useful context for Objective 2's document collection,
  since a council's stated purpose for signing (surfaced in the agenda item
  that records the decision) may help explain why change did or did not
  follow. **Adaptation needed:** use as background interpretation, not as a
  scored coding item.

- **S1 (Ruiz-Campillo, Castan Broto and Westman 2021)**, the motivations
  and intended-outcomes framework, reads the declaration's own wording for
  stated intent. Its method (textual analysis of the instrument's own
  wording) is directly reusable for reading WALGA's Declaration text
  itself, but its conclusion, that declarations function mainly as
  awareness-raising symbols, was drawn from a corpus of emergency
  declarations and should not be assumed to hold for a differently worded,
  non-emergency instrument without checking.

## What the two adjacent, non-declaration studies contribute

- **S7 (Krause 2011)** is the best available precedent for an instrument
  that, like WALGA's Declaration, is a voluntary commitment rather than an
  emergency declaration: joining the Cities for Climate Protection network.
  Its finding, that network membership is a weak predictor of actual
  implementation and that internal policy entrepreneurs matter more than
  the commitment itself, is a live hypothesis for this project rather than
  a settled conclusion: it says a WALGA signatory's before/after change, if
  any, may depend more on who inside the council champions it than on the
  act of signing. Its index-building method (score policies by presence of
  specific implemented items, not by the commitment's own wording) is the
  direct methodological ancestor of the coding framework below.

- **S8 (Schulze 2024)** supplies the confound list Objective 3's comparison
  group exists to guard against: autonomous change, collaborative and
  exemplary diffusion between neighbouring councils, persuasive advocacy,
  organised diffusion through a peer network (WALGA itself is exactly this
  kind of network), and funded diffusion through state or federal grant
  programs. A councils that never signed WALGA's Declaration could still
  show policy change through any of these channels, which is precisely why
  Objective 3 cannot be skipped: without it, a state-wide diffusion effect
  could be misread as a Declaration effect.

## Net conclusion for the coding framework

No included framework can be applied to WALGA's Declaration unmodified.
The transferable parts are: Bush and Doyon's five follow-up-plan elements
(minus the standalone-plan precondition), Greenfield, Moloney and
Granberg's 10-attribute checklist (minus the emergency-mode pass/fail bar),
Gudde et al.'s delivery-plan completeness check, and Krause's presence-of-
implemented-items scoring logic. Hicks et al.'s before/after comparison
design is the right shape but the wrong construct. Howarth, Lane and
Fankhauser's pathway/purpose framework and Ruiz-Campillo, Castan Broto and
Westman's motivations/intended-outcomes reading are useful interpretive
context, not scored items. Schulze's diffusion channels are the confound
checklist Objective 3 needs to name explicitly. `coding-framework.md` sets
out how these pieces combine into the scheme used in Objectives 2 and 3.
